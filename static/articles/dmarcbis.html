DMARCbis

The protocol upgrade that retires the Public Suffix List from the spec

I need to tell you something mildly absurd about your email security.

For the past decade, your DMARC record has leaned on a text file maintained by volunteers on GitHub. Not a standard. Not a protocol. A list. Curated by humans who cared enough to track every top-level domain, every private suffix, every odd little corner of the internet's naming system. For free.

It sounds like a vulnerability. Somehow it became a quiet triumph instead.

Since its inception, DMARC has relied on the Public Suffix List to determine the organizational domain used for policy discovery and identifier alignment. The PSL started as a 2007 Mozilla Foundation project, back when "the internet" still felt like a collection of people who showed up to help. Over time it became the bedrock of global email security. A workaround that became permanent, that nobody planned for and nobody could remove.

Receivers reference it to answer a question that sounds simple but isn't.

Where does your organizational domain end?

Take mail.example.co.uk. Is the organizational domain example.co.uk or co.uk? Get that wrong, and DMARC policy silently misses the message. No error. No alert. Just a gap where protection was supposed to be. That question is the load-bearing decision, and for a decade, a volunteer text file held the answer.

The remarkable part is not that this existed. It is that it worked.

Those volunteers tracked new TLDs, private suffixes, and the internet's edge cases, quietly and accurately, for free. No headlines. No thank-yous from the platforms that depended on them. They just kept the list current while everything around it scaled. Most of those volunteers are unknown to the industry that depends on them.

DMARC depended on something it did not own. It held anyway.

You made it work anyway

Here is the part that matters more than the mechanism.

RFC 7489 was not a Standards Track document. It was published as Informational, on the Independent Submission stream, which means it didn't even go through IETF working group consensus. In IETF terms, that is a polite way of saying "here are some ideas, good luck."

Vendors had room to interpret. And some of them did. Creatively.

But operators did not wait for permission.

They implemented DMARC anyway. They compared notes in forums and working groups. They built tooling. They debugged alignment failures, squinted at aggregate reports until the XML made sense, and taught each other what they learned. Communities like the Messaging, Malware and Mobile Anti-Abuse Working Group turned a loose document and a volunteer list into the actual floor of email security.

If you are the person who set your domain's DMARC record, you might not have been in those forums debugging alignment failures. You might have inherited a record someone else configured. That is fine. You still benefited from the people who were in the trenches, and their work is why p=reject is something receivers actually act on.

Then Gmail and Yahoo required DMARC for bulk senders in 2024, because they had spam problems they wanted to solve. The community had been building the foundation for years. The mandate finished a job operators had already started.

You are part of that, whether you set the record yourself or inherited it from someone who cared before you did.

A decade of volunteer-maintained infrastructure held. It was also a single point of failure.

The fix is specified. Deployment will take time.

DMARCbis removes the Public Suffix List from the critical path.

In its place is the DNS tree walk.

Instead of referencing a list, the receiver queries DNS directly. It starts at the full domain in the message and walks upward one label at a time, checking for a DMARC record at each step:

The DNS itself answers the question no list ever fully could. No download. No cache staleness. No volunteer keeping a text file current at 11pm because a new TLD launched.

A DNS-based protocol finally uses DNS to answer its own question. It took a decade to get here.

This did not come from theory. The working group incorporated what operators learned in the trenches: inconsistencies in PSL handling, unreliable behavior of deprecated tags like pct=, and gaps like non-existent subdomain spoofing, which the np= tag now addresses.

Now the honest part: as of 2026, most receivers still evaluate DMARC using RFC 7489 behavior. Tree walk support is rolling out, slowly.

The main specification has been approved by the IESG. Final RFC publication is held pending the companion failure-reporting document, likely landing in 2026 or early 2027. After that, receivers need to implement the tree walk in their parsers. That will be gradual. Nobody flips a switch on global email infrastructure over a weekend. If someone tells you otherwise, ask questions.

The Public Suffix List is not disappearing overnight. The record you have today continues to work, and it will continue to work throughout the transition. You do not need to panic. You do not even need to rush. You just need to know what is coming.

What you actually need to do

The heavy lift is on the receiver side. On your side, there is a short list of straightforward edits. The kind you can do in one sitting.

  1. Remove deprecated tags. pct=, rf=, and ri= are going away. Most were never handled consistently anyway, so you are not losing capability you were reliably using. Clean them out.
  2. Fix reporting URIs. Make sure they begin with mailto:. The new parser is stricter. This is the sort of thing that either works or silently fails, and "silently fails" is not the DMARC experience anyone wants.
  3. Understand psd=. The tag has three values: y (you are a public suffix domain), n (you are an organizational domain), and u (unknown, let the tree walk decide). When the tag is absent, dmarcbis treats it as u and resolves your domain via the tree walk procedure. Most operators do not need to publish psd= at all. If you run a registry or a public suffix domain, declare psd=y. Otherwise the tree walk will correctly identify you as the organizational domain.
  4. Consider np=reject. If you are already at p=reject, this closes the non-existent subdomain spoofing gap. Audit third-party senders first. The tag is optional, but the protection is real.
  5. Verify external reporting authorization. If rua= points to a third party, that destination must publish a special record to confirm it is authorized to receive your reports. Without it, reports silently fail.

That is the list. Minutes, not days. The hard part, deploying the tree walk across the world's mail systems, is not yours to carry. You just need to tidy up your side of the fence.
