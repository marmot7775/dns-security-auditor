DMARCbis: The Next DMARC Standard

DMARCbis replaces RFC 7489 with stricter parsing, new subdomain protections, and a DNS-native discovery mechanism. Here's what changes, why it matters, and how to prepare.

What is DMARCbis?

DMARCbis is the IETF's update to the DMARC email authentication standard. It was approved by the IESG in 2025 and replaces the original RFC 7489 (published in 2015 as an Informational RFC) with a Proposed Standard, a significant upgrade in authority. The name stands for "DMARC bis" (Latin for "twice" or "again"), signaling it's the second iteration of the specification.

The update addresses real-world problems discovered over a decade of DMARC deployment: inconsistent parsing across receivers, unreliable percentage-based policy rollout, no protection for non-existent subdomains, and dependence on the manually-maintained Public Suffix List for determining organizational domain boundaries.

Critically, DMARCbis maintains backward compatibility. It still uses v=DMARC1 as the version identifier, and existing DMARC records continue to work. But records that relied on lenient parsing or deprecated features will encounter issues as receivers adopt the stricter specification.

New Tags Explained

np= NEW in DMARCbis

Non-Existent Subdomain Policy. Controls what happens when someone sends email from a subdomain that doesn't exist in DNS, like secure-login.yourdomain.com. Attackers prefer these because they look convincing and most organizations don't realize they need protection for domains that aren't real.

Values: none, quarantine, reject. Falls back to sp=, then p= if not set.

Adoption: 0% of top 1,000 domains.

psd= NEW in DMARCbis

Public Suffix Domain. Declares whether a domain is a Public Suffix (like .com or .gov.uk). This tells the DNS tree walk where to stop. If your domain is NOT a public suffix (most aren't), set psd=n. This replaces the PSL, a manually-maintained list that was the source of inconsistencies across receivers.

Adoption: 0.1% of top 1,000 domains (1 domain).

t= NEW in DMARCbis

Test Mode. A binary testing flag that replaces the unreliable pct tag. When t=y is set, your published policy drops one level: p=reject becomes p=quarantine, p=quarantine becomes p=none. This gives you a safe, predictable way to test a stricter policy before committing.

Under RFC 7489, pct=50 meant "apply policy to 50% of failing mail," but receivers implemented this inconsistently.

Adoption: 0% of top 1,000 domains.

Deprecated Tags

pct= DEPRECATED

Replaced by t=y. Only values of 0 and 100 were reliably honored by receivers. If your record has pct=, remove it and use t=y if you need test mode, or nothing for full enforcement.

rf= DEPRECATED

Only afrf (Authentication Failure Reporting Format) was ever implemented. The tag served no practical purpose.

ri= DEPRECATED

Reporting intervals were rarely respected by receivers. DMARCbis standardizes daily aggregate reports. Safe to remove.

The DNS Tree Walk

Under RFC 7489, if you queried DMARC for news.marketing.example.com, the resolver would check two places: the exact domain (_dmarc.news.marketing.example.com) and the organizational domain (_dmarc.example.com), determined by the Public Suffix List.

DMARCbis replaces this with a DNS Tree Walk that checks every level:

_dmarc.news.marketing.example.com No record
_dmarc.marketing.example.com No record
_dmarc.example.com Record found
_dmarc.com Stopped by psd=

The first record found becomes the effective policy. This means a DMARC record at an intermediate level like _dmarc.marketing.example.com is found by DMARCbis but invisible to RFC 7489. This can produce different effective policies depending on which standard the receiver uses.

The tree walk has a safety limit of 8 DNS queries to prevent abuse.

However, some receivers are already implementing DMARCbis features. Major German inbox providers including GMX, mail.com, and WEB.DE are running DMARCbis in production and sending aggregate reports following the updated specification.

The standard has been approved by the IESG and is awaiting final publication. The failure-reporting document is the last blocking item. When it publishes, adoption will accelerate, especially if major providers like Gmail, Microsoft, and Yahoo announce enforcement timelines, similar to how Google and Yahoo's 2024 bulk sender requirements doubled DMARC adoption overnight.

How to Prepare

The good news: preparing for DMARCbis is safe to do today. All changes are backward-compatible with RFC 7489.

  1. Check your current record with dns-audit.com
  2. Fix any structural issues flagged by strict validation
  3. Remove deprecated tags (pct, rf, ri)
  4. Add np=reject if your root policy is reject
  5. Add psd=n (unless you're actually a public suffix domain)
  6. Use t=y instead of pct for testing
  7. Ensure all rua=/ruf= URIs have the mailto: prefix
  8. Re-check to verify DMARCbis Ready status

Your target record should look something like:

v=DMARC1; p=reject; sp=reject; np=reject; psd=n; fo=1; rua=mailto:dmarc@yourdomain.com
