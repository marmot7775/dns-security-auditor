DMARCbis: The Next DMARC Standard

DMARCbis replaces RFC 7489 with stricter parsing, new subdomain protections, and DNS-based domain discovery. Here is what changed and what it means for your domain.

What is DMARCbis?

DMARCbis is the IETF's update to the DMARC email authentication standard. It replaces the original RFC 7489, published in 2015 as an Informational RFC, with a Standards Track specification. That status change matters: it signals that DMARC is no longer an experimental protocol but a formal standard with stronger expectations for vendor compliance.

The name stands for "DMARC bis" (Latin for "twice" or "again"). It is the second iteration of the specification, and it also obsoletes RFC 9091, which defined Public Suffix Domain DMARC policies. RFC 9091 (published in 2021) was an experimental standard that defined how public suffix domains could publish DMARC policies. DMARCbis incorporates RFC 9091's goals through the psd= tag while providing a more robust mechanism via the DNS tree walk. With DMARCbis, RFC 9091 is no longer needed and is formally obsoleted.

The update addresses problems that surfaced over a decade of real-world DMARC deployment. Receivers parsed records inconsistently. The percentage-based rollout tag was unreliable. Non-existent subdomains had no dedicated protection. And organizational domain boundaries depended entirely on the Public Suffix List, a manually-maintained external file that different receivers updated on different schedules.

DMARCbis maintains backward compatibility. It still uses v=DMARC1 as the version tag, and existing DMARC records continue to work. But records that relied on lenient parsing or deprecated features will need attention as receivers adopt the stricter rules.

Why DMARCbis?

DMARC was published in 2015 as an Informational RFC. It worked, mostly, but a decade of deployment exposed real problems that could not be fixed with implementation guidance alone. The IETF working group spent several years working through these issues. Here is what they were solving.

Subdomain spoofing had no answer. RFC 7489 let you set a policy for your domain and a subdomain policy with sp=. But attackers learned to spoof from subdomains that do not exist in DNS, like secure-login.yourbank.com. There was no tag to address this. DMARCbis adds np= to let domain owners set a policy specifically for non-existent subdomains.

The Public Suffix List was a single point of fragility. DMARC needed to know where one organization's domain ends and the public suffix begins. It relied on the PSL, a community-maintained text file hosted on GitHub. Different receivers updated their copy on different schedules. A new TLD could go weeks without being recognized. DMARCbis replaces this with the DNS Tree Walk, where receivers walk up the DNS hierarchy and domain owners declare their own boundaries with the psd= tag. The answer lives in DNS, not in a file someone has to remember to update.

Percentage rollout was unreliable. The pct= tag was meant to let domain owners gradually enforce their policy on a percentage of failing mail. In practice, receivers implemented it inconsistently. Some honored it exactly, some rounded, some ignored it entirely. A domain owner setting pct=50 had no reliable way to predict what would happen. DMARCbis replaces this with t=y, a simple binary: you are either testing or you are enforcing. No ambiguity.

Parsing rules were too loose. RFC 7489 was lenient about record syntax. Some receivers accepted malformed records that others rejected, which meant the same DMARC record could produce different results depending on who was reading it. DMARCbis tightens parsing so that a valid record is valid everywhere and a broken record fails everywhere.

The standard needed real authority. As an Informational RFC, DMARC was technically a suggestion. Vendors could implement it however they saw fit, and they did. Promoting DMARC to Standards Track gives the specification formal weight and sets clearer expectations for how receivers should behave.

DMARCbis restricts SPF alignment to MAIL FROM only, eliminating ambiguity. New Tags Explained

np= NEW in DMARCbis

Non-Existent Subdomain Policy. This tag controls what happens when someone sends email from a subdomain that does not exist in DNS, like secure-login.yourdomain.com or invoice.yourdomain.com. Attackers prefer non-existent subdomains because they look legitimate and most organizations do not realize they need protection for domains that are not real.

Values: none, quarantine, reject. Policy for non-existent subdomains follows a defined fallback chain: np= (if present) takes precedence. If np= is absent, sp= applies. If sp= is also absent, p= is used. This hierarchy allows administrators to set a strict reject policy for non-existent subdomains (via np=reject) while maintaining a more permissive policy for legitimate subdomains (via sp=none or sp=quarantine).

Before adding np=reject, review whether any third-party services send mail from dynamically generated subdomains on your behalf. An np=reject policy applies to all non-existent subdomains without exception.

psd= NEW in DMARCbis

Public Suffix Domain indicator. Declares whether a domain is a Public Suffix Domain (like .com, .co.uk, or .gov.au) so the DNS tree walk knows where to stop.

Three values: psd=y means the domain is a PSD and subdomains should be treated as separate organizational domains. psd=n means the domain is not a PSD and is the organizational domain for itself and its subdomains (the walk stops here). psd=u (the default when the tag is absent) means the domain has not declared whether it is a public suffix. The tree walk continues upward to find a domain that explicitly declares psd=y (this is a public suffix) or psd=n (this is not a public suffix, stop here). If neither is found within 8 queries, DMARC does not apply.

Most domains should use psd=n. Only operators of actual public suffixes (registries, ccTLDs) should use psd=y. If you are unsure, psd=n is almost certainly correct for your domain.

t= NEW in DMARCbis

Test mode flag. Replaces the deprecated pct= tag. When t=y is set, the domain owner signals that they are testing their policy. Receivers should apply special handling rather than enforcing the stated policy on failing messages.

The key difference from pct=: the old percentage tag told receivers to apply the full policy to a random sample of failing mail. Receivers implemented this inconsistently, with some ignoring it entirely and others interpreting the percentage differently. The binary t= flag removes that ambiguity. Either you are testing (t=y) or you are enforcing (t=n, or omit the tag).

When t= is absent or set to n, the published policy applies as stated.

Deprecated Tags

pct= DEPRECATED

Replaced by the t= tag. In practice, only values of 0 and 100 were reliably honored. Intermediate values like pct=50 produced unpredictable behavior because receivers implemented percentage sampling differently. If your record includes pct=, remove it. Use t=y if you need test mode, or omit the tag entirely for full enforcement.

rf= DEPRECATED

Report format. Only afrf (Authentication Failure Reporting Format) was ever implemented by any receiver. The tag served no practical purpose and can be safely removed.

ri= DEPRECATED

Reporting interval. Receivers rarely respected custom intervals. DMARCbis standardizes on daily aggregate reports. Safe to remove.

The DNS Tree Walk

Under RFC 7489, DMARC policy discovery checked exactly two locations: the exact domain and the organizational domain (determined by the Public Suffix List). If you queried DMARC for news.marketing.example.com, the resolver would check _dmarc.news.marketing.example.com and _dmarc.example.com. Nothing in between.

DMARCbis replaces this with a DNS Tree Walk. Starting at the exact domain, the resolver walks up the DNS hierarchy one label at a time, querying for a DMARC record at each level:

_dmarc.news.marketing.example.com No record
_dmarc.marketing.example.com No record
_dmarc.example.com Record found
_dmarc.com Stopped (psd=n at example.com)

The first record found becomes the effective policy. The walk stops when it hits a domain with psd=y or psd=n, or after a maximum of 8 DNS queries (a safety limit to prevent abuse from deeply nested domain names).

If no DMARC record is found after 8 queries, or if the walk reaches the DNS root without encountering a record with psd=y or psd=n, DMARC evaluation concludes that no policy applies. The domain is treated as having no DMARC record, and receivers apply their default handling.

This matters because a DMARC record published at an intermediate level, like _dmarc.marketing.example.com, is discovered by DMARCbis but invisible to RFC 7489. During the transition period where some receivers use the old algorithm and some use the new one, the same domain can have different effective policies depending on which standard the receiver implements.

What This Means for Your Domain

The changes in DMARCbis are backward-compatible. Your existing DMARC record with v=DMARC1 will continue to work. But there are things worth reviewing, and what matters depends on your specific configuration.

The simplest approach: run an audit on your domain. The results include a DMARCbis readiness assessment that checks your record against the updated specification and flags exactly what applies to you.

The short version

For most domains, the key changes are: remove pct=, rf=, and ri= if present, since these tags are deprecated and will be ignored. Add np=reject if you already enforce p=reject and want to close the non-existent subdomain loophole. Add psd=n to declare that your domain is not a public suffix. And confirm that all rua= and ruf= URIs include the mailto: prefix, since bare email addresses are no longer accepted.

A word of caution on np=: this tag applies to all non-existent subdomains. If you use services that send email from dynamically created subdomains, or if marketing teams spin up campaign-specific subdomains without creating DNS records first, an np=reject policy will cause that mail to fail DMARC. Audit your sending infrastructure before adding this tag.

An example of a DMARCbis-compliant record:

v=DMARC1; p=reject; sp=reject; np=reject; psd=n; fo=1; rua=mailto:dmarc@yourdomain.com
