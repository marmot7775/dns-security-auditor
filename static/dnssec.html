DNSSEC stopped being hard

The reputation did not catch up. The DNSSEC you remember required hands-on work at every step. The DNSSEC you can deploy at a major provider in 2026 is a checkbox.

You have an opinion about DNSSEC. I did too. There's a decent chance both of us formed it before 2017 and never had a reason to revisit it.

Mine: too much operational risk for the threat it closed. KSK rollovers that broke domains. Registrars that wouldn't accept DS updates without a ticket. Algorithm choices that pushed responses into TCP fallback. I read "Against DNSSEC" when it came out. I cited it. The IANIX outage list was earned. Paul Vixie publicly soured on the adoption trajectory and the criticism stuck. None of that was wrong at the time.

What was wrong was that I left the opinion there and never came back to check on it.

This piece is the check I finally did. It walks through what changed, what DNSSEC actually protects against, where it still falls short, and what good deployment looks like now. The honest scope: less than the marketing says, more than the skeptics concede.

If you get to the end and decide DNSSEC is not for you, that's fine. I'm not here to convert anyone. I'd just rather you keep your skepticism for current reasons than for ten-year-old ones.

The thing that quietly got fixed

The DNSSEC you remember required hands-on work at every step. Sign your zone. Get the DS record from your DNS host into your registrar's web form. Keep it there through every key rollover. Forget the registrar step on a KSK rotation and your domain went dark for every recursive resolver doing validation. By the late 2010s, that was a meaningful slice of the public internet.

The math was always fine. The operations were the problem.

Three RFCs closed the operations problem. None of them went viral. All of them mattered.

RFC 7344, back in 2014, introduced CDS and CDNSKEY records. The idea is the kind that seems obvious after someone else thinks of it: your DNS host publishes "here is my new DS record" directly in your zone, and the registrar picks it up automatically. No web form. No 2am copy-paste session. No forgetting which registrar has which login.

RFC 8078 followed in 2017, extending CDS/CDNSKEY to cover initial DS provisioning and removal of a secure entry point, not just rollover. Most major TLD registries now run CDS scanners that pick those signals up automatically.

Then in July 2024, RFC 9615 closed the bootstrap problem: how to enable DNSSEC for the first time when no chain of trust yet exists. This was the chicken-and-egg case that had kept the whole thing manual for years. Solved.

Algorithm 13, ECDSA P-256, became the default. It's defined in RFC 6605 from 2012, but it took most of a decade for providers to make it standard. RSA-SHA256 still works, but ECDSA signatures are small enough to fit in a UDP response without forcing TCP fallback. A meaningful share of the "DNSSEC broke my domain" stories came from exactly that: the fallback.

CSYNC, RFC 7477, sits next to CDS and does the same job for NS and glue records. The child zone announces, the parent syncs. No human in the loop.

Add it up. The DNSSEC you remember was a hands-on process run through a registrar UI you had to remember the password for. The DNSSEC you can deploy at a major provider in 2026 is a checkbox. Cloudflare made it a checkbox. deSEC made it a checkbox. Route 53, NS1, Google Cloud DNS: all checkboxes.

Most of this happened in the last six years. Most of it was boring. None of it generated a viral blog post called "Actually, Maybe DNSSEC Is Fine Now," because nobody writes those. Slow, incremental improvement is the worst possible content for the internet to surface.

What it actually protects against

Most DNSSEC posts will tell you it "secures DNS." That sentence is doing a lot of work. Too much, honestly.

Here's what DNSSEC actually does. It signs DNS answers. When your resolver asks "what's the IP for example.com," DNSSEC lets it verify that the answer really came from example.com's zone and wasn't changed along the way. Origin authentication. Integrity. That's the whole job.

That's not nothing. Cache poisoning is real. The Kaminsky attack of 2008 was real. On-path DNS tampering happens on coffee shop wifi, on cut-rate ISPs, and at the borders of entire countries. If your DANE or SSHFP records have ever done anything useful, DNSSEC is why they weren't lies.

Now here's what DNSSEC does not do, despite what some marketing copy implies.

It does not stop phishing. An attacker can register paypa1.com, sign it perfectly, and your validator will happily confirm the answer came from paypa1.com. DNSSEC has no opinion on whether you should have typed that URL.

It does not stop email spoofing. That's SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. All three sit above DNSSEC and have nothing to do with it.

It does not encrypt your DNS queries. That's DoH or DoT. Different protocol, different problem. Often confused.

It does not protect against an attacker who compromises your DNS provider's signing infrastructure. If they control the signer, they can sign whatever they want.

It does not protect against an attacker who hijacks your domain at the registrar. They can change the DS record, and then they're the legitimate chain of trust.

The honest pitch is narrower than the marketing pitch and stronger because of it. DNSSEC closes a specific class of DNS-layer attacks that no other protocol closes. If you don't deploy it, you're trusting every resolver and every network between you and your authoritative server. Some of them are not honest.

Where it still falls short

Adoption depends on what you measure. Signed zones in the major TLDs sit in the low single digits of registered domains. Verisign's quarterly Domain Name Industry Brief tracks DNSSEC signing rates for .com and .net; the share has been creeping up but remains low. APNIC Labs measures resolver-side validation closer to a third of queries globally, climbing year over year. Both numbers are moving up. Signing is the slower one. Real progress. Real ceiling.

The story underneath that number is more interesting than the number itself. Adoption isn't really a domain-owner decision. It's a provider decision. The domain owner mostly finds out which side they're on after the fact.

Think back to HTTPS adoption a decade ago. Site operators didn't wake up one morning and decide they wanted TLS certificates. They'd been told for years that HTTPS was important. Most of them filed it under "yes, eventually." Then Let's Encrypt launched. Cloudflare flipped TLS on by default. Hosting providers started auto-provisioning certificates at signup. The question stopped being "do you want HTTPS?" and started being "do you even know whether your site is on HTTPS?" Most operators just woke up one day to find their provider had turned it on.

DNSSEC is in the early innings of the same transition. The providers who automated it have high adoption among their customers. The providers who didn't, don't.

Here's the practical picture.

If your DNS is at Cloudflare, deSEC, Route 53, NS1, or Google Cloud DNS, you can have DNSSEC in a few clicks. The provider handles signing, key rollover, and DS record submission to the registrar, assuming the registrar supports CDS scanning. Most major ones now do.

If your DNS is at a budget tier of a registrar that doesn't list DNSSEC as a feature, or at a generic shared-hosting setup, you have a problem. Not a hard problem. A "you might need to switch DNS hosting" problem. That's real friction. No point pretending it isn't.

If your TLD is .com, .net, .org, .io, .dev, .app, or any major ccTLD, the registry side is fine. CDS scanning works. The bottleneck is your registrar.

If your TLD is one of the long-tail TLDs with weak DNSSEC support, the bottleneck is the registry. Some TLDs still have incomplete automation. Check before assuming. The IANA TLD report is the canonical reference.

The honest summary: at a major provider on a major TLD, deploying DNSSEC in 2026 is straightforward. If you're not at one of those, you might need to migrate something before the checkbox is available to you. The protocol is no longer the bottleneck. The provider lottery still is.

What good looks like

You can tell within thirty seconds whether someone deployed DNSSEC well or just deployed DNSSEC. Bad DNSSEC is signed. Good DNSSEC is signed and survives Tuesday.

Algorithm 13, ECDSA P-256. Not RSA-SHA256. Not RSA-SHA1. Not whatever your DNS provider was defaulting to in 2014. ECDSA signatures are compact enough to avoid the fragmentation and TCP-fallback problems that gave DNSSEC its reputation for breaking things. If you're signing fresh in 2026 and your provider still defaults to RSA, ask them why.

Automated key rollover via CDS and CDNSKEY. If your DNSSEC deployment requires a human to copy a DS record from one panel to another, you don't have a deployment. You have a future incident with a date attached. The whole point of the last decade of standards work was to make that part go away.

A monitored DS record at the registrar. Not a one-time check. Continuous. The failure mode of DNSSEC isn't "wrong key." It's "key that used to be right." If your DS drifts out of sync with your zone, your domain goes dark for every validating resolver. Pingdom doesn't catch this. Most uptime monitors don't. You need something that actually validates the chain.

End-to-end validation tested, not assumed. Run DNSViz on your domain. Run Verisign's DNSSEC analyzer. Run dns-audit.com. Each of these tools will tell you whether your chain of trust is intact from root to your zone, or whether you have a quiet break that will get loud the next time a resolver tightens its validation strictness.

Hosted signing, not hand-rolled. Unless you're running DNS for a registry, a TLD, or a national CERT, you shouldn't be running your own signers. The major providers run signers at a scale and reliability you won't match in-house, and they're the ones who made RFC 9615 actually work in production.

A KSK rollover plan you've actually rehearsed. Once. Just once. Most operators have never rolled their KSK, which means the first time will be in production, under pressure, with a registrar interface they haven't looked at in three years. Pick a quarter. Do a rollover. Take notes. Now it's muscle memory.

That's the bar. Most domains that "have DNSSEC" are signed and that's roughly where the work stopped. Signed is the floor. Signed, automated, monitored, hosted, validated, and rehearsed is the ceiling. Anything less is a domain that works until it doesn't.

The closer

Most posts about DNSSEC tell you to deploy it because deployment numbers must go up, or to skip it because deployment numbers prove it failed. Neither take is honest. Both treat DNSSEC like a cause.

DNSSEC is not a cause. It's infrastructure.

You enable it where it makes sense. You skip it where it doesn't.

If you run a bank, a registrar, a CA, a software update channel, a government domain, or anything where a DNS hijack ends up on a regulator's desk: deploy it, deploy it well, deploy it to the bar above. The threat model is real and nothing else closes it.

If you run a marketing site, a personal blog, or a domain that mostly redirects somewhere else: sign it if your provider makes it easy, skip it if they don't. The threat model doesn't justify migrating DNS hosting for a brochure site. Don't feel bad about that call.

Here's the one thing I do want you to stop doing: repeating ten-year-old talking points as if nothing changed.

The "DNSSEC is too complicated" line was true in 2014. It's mostly false in 2026 if you're at a provider that bothered.

The "DNSSEC doesn't even encrypt anything" line confuses the threat model with someone else's threat model.

The "nobody uses it" line ignores that the people who need it most are quietly using it. You just don't hear from them.

Give it a fresh look. Run DNSViz, Verisign's DNSSEC analyzer, or dns-audit.com on a domain you care about and see what the chain actually looks like. Then decide.

Most domains land in one of three places. Signed and clean. Signed but broken at the registrar handoff. Unsigned because the provider doesn't offer it. All three are useful answers. None of them is the answer you formed in 2014.
