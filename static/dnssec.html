DNSSEC stopped being hard

The reputation did not catch up. The DNSSEC you remember required hands-on work at every step. The DNSSEC you can deploy at a major provider in 2026 is a checkbox.

You have an opinion about DNSSEC. There is a decent chance you formed it before 2017 and have not had a reason to revisit it since.

Mine looked like this. Complicated. Fragile. The kind of protocol that takes your domain offline at 2am, the kind you turn on when a regulator makes you and skip otherwise. I read Against DNSSEC when it came out. I cited it more than once. The skepticism I absorbed in the rough years was earned. The IANIX outage list was earned. Paul Vixie publicly soured on DNSSEC's adoption trajectory and the criticism stuck. None of that was wrong.

What was wrong was that I left the opinion there and stopped checking on it.

This piece is the check. A walkthrough of what changed, what DNSSEC actually does (less than its supporters claim, more than its critics admit), where it still falls short, and what good deployment looks like in 2026.

If at the end of it you decide DNSSEC is not for you, that is fine. I am not in the business of converting anyone. I would just rather you keep your skepticism for current reasons than for ten-year-old ones.

The thing that quietly got fixed

The protocol you remember required hands-on work at every step. Sign your zone. Get the DS record from your DNS host into your registrar's web form. Keep it there through every key rollover. Forget the registrar step on a KSK rotation and the domain went dark for every recursive resolver doing validation, which by the late 2010s was a meaningful slice of the public internet. The math was always fine. The operations were the problem.

Three RFCs closed the operations problem.

RFC 7344 (2014) introduced CDS and CDNSKEY records. Translation: your DNS host can publish "here is my new DS record" directly in your zone, and the registrar can pick it up automatically. No more web form. No more 2am.

RFC 8078 (2017) formalized how parent zones should accept those changes. Most major TLDs adopted it.

RFC 9615 (July 2024) closed the bootstrap problem: how to enable DNSSEC for the first time when no chain of trust yet exists. This was the chicken-and-egg case that had kept DNSSEC manual for years.

Algorithm 13 (ECDSA P-256, RFC 6605) became the default. RSA-SHA256 still works, but ECDSA signatures are small enough to fit in a UDP response without forcing TCP fallback, which is where a meaningful share of "DNSSEC broke my domain" stories actually came from.

CSYNC (RFC 7477) sits next to CDS, doing the same job for NS and glue records. The child zone announces, the parent syncs, no human in the loop.

Add it up. The DNSSEC you remember was a manual sport played with a registrar UI you had to remember the password for. The DNSSEC you can deploy at a major provider in 2026 is a checkbox. Cloudflare made it a checkbox. deSEC made it a checkbox. Route 53, NS1, Google Cloud DNS, all checkboxes.

Most of this happened in the last six years. Most of it was boring. None of it generated a viral blog post called "Actually, Maybe DNSSEC Is Fine Now," because nobody writes those, because slow incremental improvement is the worst possible content for the internet to surface.

What it actually protects against

Most DNSSEC posts will tell you it "secures DNS." That sentence is doing a lot of work. Most of the work is dishonest.

Here is what DNSSEC actually does. It signs DNS answers. When your resolver asks "what is the IP for example.com," DNSSEC lets it verify the answer came from example.com's zone and was not changed in transit. Origin authentication. Integrity. That is the whole job.

That is not nothing. Cache poisoning is real. The Kaminsky attack of 2008 was real. On-path DNS tampering happens on coffee shop wifi, on cut-rate ISPs, and at the borders of entire countries. If your DANE or SSHFP records have ever done anything useful, DNSSEC is why they were not lies.

Here is what DNSSEC does not do, despite what some marketing copy implies.

It does not stop phishing. An attacker can register paypa1.com, sign it perfectly, and your validator will happily confirm the answer came from paypa1.com. DNSSEC has no opinion on whether you should have typed that URL.

It does not stop email spoofing. That is SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, all of which sit above DNSSEC and have nothing to do with it.

It does not encrypt your DNS queries. That is DoH or DoT. Different protocol, different problem, often confused.

It does not protect against an attacker who compromises your DNS provider's signing infrastructure. They can sign whatever they want.

It does not protect against an attacker who hijacks your domain at the registrar. They can change the DS record.

The honest pitch is narrower than the marketing pitch and stronger because of it. DNSSEC closes a specific class of DNS-layer attacks that no other protocol closes. If you do not deploy it, you are trusting every resolver and every network between you and your authoritative server. Some of them are not honest.

Where it still falls short

Adoption sits in the low single digits of all registered domains in early 2026, somewhere in the 4 to 5 percent range depending on the source. Up from roughly 1 percent in 2017. Real progress. Real ceiling.

The story under that number is more interesting than the number itself. Adoption is not a domain-owner decision. It is a provider decision, and the domain owner mostly finds out which side of it they are on after the fact.

Think back to HTTPS adoption a decade ago. Site operators did not wake up one morning and decide they wanted TLS certificates. They had been told for years that HTTPS was important. Most of them filed it under "yes, eventually." Then Let's Encrypt happened, and Cloudflare flipped TLS on by default, and the hosting providers started auto-provisioning certs at signup, and the question stopped being "do you want HTTPS?" and started being "do you even know whether your site is on HTTPS?" The answer for most operators was that their provider had quietly turned it on while they were not looking.

DNSSEC is in the early innings of the same transition. The providers who automated it have high adoption among their customers. The providers who did not, do not.

A practical breakdown:

If your DNS is at Cloudflare, deSEC, Route 53, NS1, or Google Cloud DNS, you can have DNSSEC in about ninety seconds. Click a button. Done. The provider handles signing, key rollover, and DS record submission to the registrar (assuming the registrar supports CDS scanning, which most major ones now do).

If your DNS is at a budget tier of a registrar that does not list DNSSEC as a feature, or at a generic shared-hosting setup, you have a problem. Not a hard problem. A "you may need to switch DNS hosting" problem. That is real friction, and the article you are reading would be lying to claim otherwise.

If your TLD is .com, .net, .org, .io, .dev, .app, or any major ccTLD, the registry side is fine. CDS scanning works. The bottleneck is your registrar.

If your TLD is one of the long-tail TLDs with weak DNSSEC support, the bottleneck is the registry. Some TLDs still have incomplete automation, and you should check before assuming. The IANA TLD report is the canonical reference.

The summary, honestly: at a major provider on a major TLD, deploying DNSSEC in 2026 is a checkbox. If you are not at one of those, you may need to migrate something before the checkbox is available to you. The protocol is no longer the bottleneck. The provider lottery is.

What good looks like

You can tell within thirty seconds whether someone deployed DNSSEC well or just deployed DNSSEC. Bad DNSSEC is signed. Good DNSSEC is signed and survives Tuesday.

Algorithm 13 (ECDSA P-256). Not RSA-SHA256, not RSA-SHA1, not whatever your DNS provider was defaulting to in 2014. Smaller signatures, lower fragmentation risk, lower TCP-fallback risk. If you are signing fresh in 2026 and your provider still defaults to RSA, ask why.

Automated key rollover via CDS and CDNSKEY. If your DNSSEC deployment requires a human to copy a DS record from one panel to another, you do not have a deployment. You have a future incident with a date attached. The whole point of the last decade of standards work was to make that part go away.

A monitored DS record at the registrar. Not a one-time check. Continuous. The failure mode of DNSSEC is not "wrong key." It is "key that used to be right." If your DS drifts out of sync with your zone, your domain goes dark for every validating resolver. Pingdom does not catch this. Most uptime monitors do not. You need something that actually validates the chain.

End-to-end validation tested, not assumed. Run DNSViz on your domain. Run Verisign's DNSSEC analyzer. Run dns-audit.com. All three will tell you whether your chain of trust is intact from root to your zone, or whether you have a quiet break that will get loud the next time a resolver upgrades its validation strictness.

Hosted signing, not hand-rolled. Unless you are running DNS for a registry, a TLD, or a national CERT, you should not be running your own signers. The major providers run signers at a scale and reliability you will not match in-house, and they are the ones who made RFC 9615 actually work in production.

A KSK rollover plan you have actually rehearsed. Once. Just once. Most operators have never rolled their KSK, which means the first time will be in production, under pressure, with a registrar interface they have not looked at in three years. Pick a quarter. Do a rollover. Take notes. Now it is muscle memory.

That is the bar. Most domains that "have DNSSEC" are signed and that is roughly where the work stopped. Signed is the floor. Signed, automated, monitored, hosted, validated, and rehearsed is the ceiling. Anything less is a domain that works until it does not.

The story almost nobody tells

In January 2026, a researcher named Barbara Jantzen published a long dialogue with Roy Arends, who has worked on DNSSEC since the late 1990s and is now at ICANN. The piece ran on CircleID under the title The Excruciating Slow Rise of DNSSEC. It is one of the more unusual technical articles I have read in years.

It is unusual because Jantzen is not a DNS engineer. She trained in the humanities and joined deSEC, a non-profit DNSSEC operator, as a non-technical hire. What she noticed working alongside the people who had spent careers on this protocol was not technical. It was emotional. The DNSSEC engineers were carrying something. Embarrassment about how the rollout had gone. Frustration that the operational fixes from the last decade had not changed the public conversation. In some cases, something close to grief about a project they had given fifteen or twenty years to.

Jantzen tested whether the feeling was as real as it looked. She wrote a stand-up comedy set about DNSSEC and performed it at an IETF social event. By her account in the dialogue, it landed. The DNSSEC people in the room laughed at jokes about their own protocol, in front of each other, for what may have been the first time in public.

That is the part of the story almost nobody outside the DNS community knows. The protocol's reputation problem is partly a media problem and partly a human problem. The people who built it stopped expecting credit. The people who use it formed opinions in the bad years and never updated them. Jantzen's article is the first piece I have seen that names both halves at once.

It does not change the technical case for or against DNSSEC. It does change the way you might read the technical case. The thing you are skeptical of has been quietly fixed by people who stopped expecting anyone to notice.

The closer

Most posts about DNSSEC tell you to deploy it because deployment numbers must go up, or to skip it because deployment numbers prove it failed. Both takes are bad. Both treat DNSSEC like a cause.

DNSSEC is not a cause. It is infrastructure.

You enable it where it makes sense. You skip it where it does not.

If you are running a bank, a registrar, a CA, a software update channel, a government domain, or anything where a DNS hijack ends up on a regulator's desk, deploy it, deploy it well, deploy it to the bar above. The threat model is real and nothing else closes it.

If you are running a marketing site, a personal blog, or a domain that mostly redirects somewhere else, sign it if your provider makes it a checkbox, skip it if they do not. The threat model does not justify migrating DNS hosting for a brochure site, and you do not need to feel bad about that call.

What I want you to stop doing is repeating ten-year-old talking points like they are current. The "DNSSEC is too complicated" line was true in 2014 and is mostly false in 2026 if you are at a provider that bothered. The "DNSSEC doesn't even encrypt anything" line confuses the threat model with someone else's threat model. The "nobody uses it" line ignores that the people who need it most are quietly using it and you just do not hear from them, because their domains are not on fire.

Update your priors. Run dns-audit.com, DNSViz, or Verisign's analyzer on a domain you care about and see what the chain actually looks like. Then decide.

Most domains land in one of three places. Signed and clean. Signed but broken at the registrar handoff. Unsigned because the provider does not offer it. All three are useful answers. None of them is the answer you formed in 2014.
